01 — EXECUTIVE CONCLUSION

Yes, and the ceiling is higher than you assumed

On 4 GB VRAM + 12 GB RAM + NVMe, a text-only Qwen3.8-Flash-Next should reach 1–4 tok/s with 4-bit experts, and 3–8 tok/s with 2-bit experts — not 0.1 tok/s.

The hypothesis survives. Total model size is genuinely decoupled from resident working set here, for three structural reasons that I verified rather than assumed: the dense always-resident core quantizes to ~2.1 GiB and fits in VRAM outright; the 51.2B-parameter PLE table costs ~64 KiB of I/O per token despite being 28% of the model; and expert traffic is 633 MiB/token at 2-bit, before any caching, which a 3 GB/s SSD serves at 4.5 tok/s even at a 0% hit rate.

The binding constraint is expert bytes per token. Nothing else is close. Every design decision should be scored against that one number.

What "work" means, concretely, in ascending order of difficulty: correct greedy decoding with bounded residency is very likely (comparable systems already do it); 1 tok/s is likely; 2–4 tok/s is plausible and is the honest target; above 5 tok/s requires expert cache hit rates that nobody has yet measured on this specific model — which is exactly the gap your first milestone should close.

The one number that should worry you

12 GB of RAM leaves roughly 7 GiB for an expert cache after OS and runtime. That is 5,437 of 24,576 expert slots at 2-bit (22%), or 2,880 slots at 4-bit (11.7%). The 56–64% LRU hit rates measured on Qwen3-Next-80B were obtained at far higher cache-to-model ratios. Whether that locality survives at 12% capacity on a 512-expert / top-10 router is the open question, and it is cheap to answer before building anything.

04 — CORRECTIONS

Five assumptions in the brief that the checkpoint contradicts

You asked to be told when the research disagrees with the direction. It does, in five places, and two of them redirect significant effort.

Brief assumed

Stripping vision is a major win, worth a capability-analysis subsystem and selective shard downloads.

Checkpoint says

Vision is 448.9M params — 0.25% of the model, 0.84 GiB. It also lives inside a single mixed shard, so you cannot skip downloading anything. Standard GGUF conversion already splits it into a separate mmproj. This is a rounding error, not a strategy.

Brief assumed

PLE parameters are distributed across layers and need per-layer prefetch policy.

Checkpoint says

There is exactly one PLE block, at layer 1. All 51.2B parameters are one flat table consulted once per token, near the bottom of the network. This makes the prefetch problem dramatically easier than the brief supposes — see §09.

Brief assumed

Experts can be addressed as individual tensors and reordered on disk by co-activation.

Checkpoint says

Experts are stored fused: one (512,1280,2560) tensor and one (512,2560,640) per layer. Expert e's two halves are in different tensors, ~120 GB apart on disk. Repacking to [e: gate_up ‖ down] halves your read count before any clever layout work. Do that first; co-activation reordering is a later, smaller win.

Brief assumed

Extreme quantization (1-bit / 1.58-bit) is the headline lever, and the repo currently ships a ternary quantizer.

Checkpoint + literature say

Going 4-bit → 2-bit halves expert I/O and roughly doubles the throughput ceiling. Going 2-bit → 1-bit buys only a further 1.8× and Flash-MoE measured 2-bit already breaking JSON and tool calling on a sibling Qwen MoE. The residency win comes from sparsity, not bit-width. Sub-2-bit is a research branch, not the plan.

Brief assumed

A sophisticated custom expert cache (TinyLFU, SLRU, predictive promotion) is a core deliverable.

Literature says

Flash-MoE tested Metal LRU, malloc cache, and LZ4 compressed cache — every one was slower than just trusting the OS page cache, which reached ~71% hit naturally. And #27712 found LRU64 (3.79 tok/s) no better than LRU32 (3.80). Build the measurement harness before the cache; the null hypothesis is that mmap + madvise wins.

One assumption that held up better than expected

"~6B activated per token" is essentially right: I compute 6.671B, of which routed experts are 2.359B (35.4%) and the always-dense remainder is 4.312B. And your instinct that only part of the network uses sparse attention is confirmed — 36 of 48 layers are Gated DeltaNet with constant-size state, which is why KV cost here is trivial compared to a dense transformer of this scale.

Rules for the stripper

  • Safe to drop, no behavioral change: model.visual.* and mtp.*. Both are structurally disjoint — nothing in the language-model forward path reads from either. MTP is a speculative multi-token head; ordinary autoregressive decoding never invokes it. This is also what the published GGUF conversion already does.
  • Do not touch the vocabulary. Confirmed: vocab_size 248320, with image_token_id 248056, video_token_id 248057, vision_start/end 248053/248054 sitting inside the embedding matrix. Removing those rows would require renumbering and would desynchronize embed_tokens, lm_head, and the tokenizer. The cost of keeping them is 5 rows × 2560 — literally nothing. Keep them.
  • Behavior-changing, requires validation, out of scope for v1: expert pruning, head pruning, layer dropping, PLE row eviction. Do not conflate these with capability stripping. In particular, PLE rows are hash-addressed with prime moduli (§09) — dropping rows changes the hash range and corrupts every lookup.
  • Shard-skipping is not available. Of 131 shards, exactly one contains vision tensors and it is mixed with GDN and hyper-connection weights. 48 shards are pure experts and 31 are pure PLE table — that purity is useful for layout, but there is no shard you can decline to download for capability reasons.

Why not 1-bit, stated as a falsifiable claim

Flash-MoE ran the same expert-streaming design at both 4-bit and 2-bit on Qwen3.5-397B and reported: 4-bit 4.36 tok/s with "full tool calling", 2-bit 5.74 tok/s but producing \name\ instead of "name" in JSON, making tool use unreliable. That is a 32% speedup for a categorical capability loss, measured on a closely related architecture. Prediction to falsify: on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, uniform ≤2-bit experts will degrade exact-format outputs (JSON validity, tool-call schema conformance) measurably before they degrade perplexity noticeably. If your eval suite only measures perplexity you will not see this and will ship a broken model.

Where runtime-aware precision genuinely helps

Your hypothesis — precision as a function of saliency × routing frequency × tier × backend — is sound, but only the middle two terms pay here, and only after profiling. The defensible version is a two-population expert split: profile routing over a calibration corpus, keep the hot head of the distribution at 4-bit where it is read constantly, and push the cold tail to 2-bit where reads are rare and the compression buys SSD bandwidth precisely when you take a miss. Storing an expert at two precisions costs 6.5 bits/weight but lets the runtime choose per-read. Do not build this until §11's profiler tells you the distribution is actually skewed.

08 — EXPERT PAGING

Bounded residency with speculative cross-layer prefetch

The structural opportunity: the router for layer ℓ is a 512×2560 matvec on the hidden state, and it is the only thing needed to know which experts layer ℓ requires. The hidden state entering layer ℓ+1 differs from that entering ℓ by one attention block and one MoE block. So layer ℓ's post-attention hidden state is a cheap, decent predictor of layer ℓ+1's routing — one extra 1.3 MB matvec buys a full layer of I/O lead time. This is the overlap you sketched, and it is implementable because routers are tiny and permanently resident.

# ---- PocketTitan expert residency manager -------------------------------
# Invariants: |VRAM slots| and |RAM slots| are fixed at init. No unbounded growth.
# All SSD reads are issued async (io_uring on Linux / OVERLAPPED on Windows).

init:
    RAM_slots  = 7 GiB / expert_record_bytes      # 2880 @4-bit
    VRAM_slots = 0.8 GiB / expert_record_bytes    # ~320 @4-bit
    ram_cache  = SLRU(RAM_slots)   # probationary 20% / protected 80%
    vram_cache = LFU (VRAM_slots)  # promotion needs sustained reuse, not one hit
    pin(routers, dense_core, norms)               # never evictable

on_token(h):
    issue_ple_prefetch(token_id)              # §09 — exact, not speculative
    inflight = {}
    for L in 0..47:
        # ---- 1. consume the speculative prefetch issued one layer ago
        if L in inflight: await_partial(inflight[L])

        h = attention_block(L, h)                 # GDN or sparse attn, VRAM-resident

        # ---- 2. authoritative routing for THIS layer
        ids, w = top_k(router[L] @ h, k=10)       # router is fp16, always resident

        # ---- 3. speculative routing for the NEXT layer, from current h
        if L < 47:
            pred = top_m(router[L+1] @ h, m=14)   # m > k: over-fetch to absorb drift
            inflight[L+1] = async_fetch(L+1, pred - resident(L+1))

        # ---- 4. execute, placing each expert where it already lives
        acc = shared_expert(L, h)                 # always VRAM
        for (e, wt) in zip(ids, w):
            match locate(L, e):
                case VRAM: acc += wt * gpu_lowbit_ffn(vram_cache[L,e], h)
                case RAM:  acc += wt * cpu_lowbit_ffn(ram_cache[L,e], h)   # §10: cheaper than uploading
                case MISS:
                    rec = await sync_fetch(L, e)  # 2.49 MiB contiguous, 1 read
                    ram_cache.insert((L,e), rec)  # enters PROBATIONARY only
                    acc += wt * cpu_lowbit_ffn(rec, h)
            touch(L, e)                           # drives SLRU promotion + LFU counters
        h = combine(acc, h)

    # ---- 5. session adaptation, amortized
    if token_index == 64:                        # after routing profile stabilizes
        hot = top_n(session_freq, VRAM_slots)
        vram_cache.pin_promote(hot)             # one bulk upload, not per-token churn

Design decisions and why

  • SLRU, not LRU, for RAM. A cold expert fetched once should not evict a warm one. New arrivals enter probation and only reach the protected segment on a second hit. This directly targets the failure mode behind #27712's finding that LRU64 was no faster than LRU32 — a bigger plain-LRU cache just holds more one-hit wonders.
  • LFU with a promotion threshold for VRAM. Uploading over PCIe costs ~2.5 MiB of link time; it only pays if the expert is reused several times. Promote on sustained frequency, in bulk, not on demand.
  • Over-fetch on speculation (m=14 for k=10). Speculative prefetch that misses is worse than useless — it evicts. Over-fetching by 40% absorbs prediction drift; the profiler in §11 sets m empirically.
  • Never block on speculation. await_partial consumes whatever arrived and lets the rest continue in flight. A prefetch must never be able to make a token slower than no prefetch.
  • Bounded by construction. Slot counts are fixed at init from measured free memory. There is no code path that grows residency, which is the property that makes "runs in 12 GB" a guarantee rather than a hope.

Test the null hypothesis first. Before any of this: run the same workload with plain mmap + madvise(MADV_RANDOM) and let the OS page cache do it. Flash-MoE got ~71% hit that way and beat all three of its custom caches. If the OS wins at 12 GB too, the correct deliverable is a good on-disk layout plus madvise hints, and this entire manager is dead code. That is a two-day experiment and it gates weeks of work.

Every head vocabulary is a distinct prime just above 20,000,000. That is a textbook multi-probe hash: 16 independent moduli decorrelate collisions so that a token colliding in one head is almost certainly clean in the other fifteen. The practical consequence for us is the important part:

PLE prefetch is exact, not speculative

Row indices are a pure function of the last three token IDs — three multiply-mod operations against fixed constants. No hidden state, no model evaluation. So: for a prompt, all 16N rows are computable before the first layer runs. During decode, the moment token t is sampled you can issue its 16 reads, and they are not needed until layer 1 of the next forward pass — an entire pass of lead time for 16 random 4 KiB reads. This latency is fully hideable, with certainty. Contrast with expert prefetch, which is irreducibly speculative.

This is independently confirmed by measurement on this exact model: llama.cpp discussion #27864 reports the n-gram table on SSD in on-demand read mode at 36.08 tok/s decode versus 39.56 tok/s fully resident — an 8.8% cost to eliminate 26.8 GiB of RAM, with no caching beyond the filesystem page cache. Prefill suffers much more (554 → 273 tok/s, −51%) because prefill touches 16 rows per prompt token in a burst; batching and sorting those reads is the obvious fix and nobody has done it.

# ---- PLE lookup path ----------------------------------------------------
row_id(head h, tokens [t0,t1,t2]):
    x = (t0*M0) ^ (t1*M1) ^ (t2*M2)           # M = layer_multipliers
    return offsets[h] + (x mod vocab_sizes[h])

on_token_sampled(t):                          # runs at sampling time, off critical path
    rows = [row_id(h, ctx[-3:]) for h in 0..15]
    for r in rows:
        if r not in row_cache: io_uring_submit(READ, ple_fd, byte_offset(r), 4096)

at_layer_1(t):
    e = concat([dequant(row_cache[r]) for r in rows])   # 16 × 160 → 2560

On-disk layout rules

  • Separate addressable object, as you proposed — ple/table.bin + ple/index.json, not a tensor in the main package. Confirmed correct by #27864, which had to split it into its own GGUF to make offloading tractable.
  • Row-aligned, group-per-row. Quantize each 160-element row as one symmetric group with an fp16 scale: 160×3 bits + 16 = 62 bytes at 3-bit. Pad to a power of two so a row never straddles two pages. A whole row must be readable in exactly one I/O.
  • Order by head, then by row id — matching the natural offset layout. Rows within a head are hash-scattered, so there is no better locality to exploit; do not waste effort trying.
  • Batch and sort prefill reads. A 2K prompt needs 32,768 row reads. Issued sorted, in one io_uring batch with depth 128, that is a coalesced sweep instead of a random walk — the fix for the −51% prefill regression measured upstream.
  • Precision is nearly free here. At 64 KiB/token the read rate is irrelevant; choose PLE precision purely on quality. 3-bit costs 18.5 GiB, 4-bit costs 25.3 GiB. Neither affects throughput. Start at 4-bit and only shrink if disk space actually binds.
10 — COMPUTE ARCHITECTURE

CPU executes experts; GPU owns the dense spine

The PCIe arithmetic in §07 forces this split, and it is the opposite of the instinct to put everything on the GPU. For an expert already sitting in RAM, executing it on the CPU costs a 2.49 MiB streaming read at ~20 GB/s ≈ 0.12 ms. Uploading that same expert over a PCIe 3.0 ×4 laptop link costs ~0.78 ms before any compute happens. CPU-resident expert execution is ~6× cheaper than shipping the weights to the GPU, and the gap widens on narrower links.

Kernels

Do not write your own from scratch, and do not dequantize to fp16 before multiplying — that is the anti-pattern you correctly identified, and it converts a bandwidth win into a bandwidth loss. Two concrete techniques from the measured systems:

  • CPU: LUT-based low-bit GEMV (T-MAC style). For W2A8/W4A8 at batch 1, precompute partial products into a lookup table and replace multiply-accumulate with table lookups plus adds. This is the right primitive for the expert path, where the weights are low-bit and the activation is a single vector.
  • GPU: fuse dequant into the FMA. Flash-MoE measured a 12% gain purely by rewriting (nibble*scale + bias)*x as fma(nibble, scale*x, bias*x), precomputing scale*x and bias*x so the FMA unit does dequantization and multiplication in one instruction. Cheap, and it applies directly to any CUDA port.
  • Keep the GDN recurrence on BLAS. The 48-head × 128×128 fp32 state update is a sgemv/sger pattern; Flash-MoE reported 64% over scalar code by calling into a tuned BLAS. Do not hand-roll it.

An advantage your hardware has over every system cited here

Flash-MoE's headline constraint was that on Apple Silicon, SSD DMA and GPU compute contend for one memory controller, so the pipeline had to be serial: GPU → SSD → GPU. On x86 with a discrete GPU, NVMe DMA lands in system RAM over its own PCIe lanes while the GPU works out of VRAM. You can genuinely overlap expert I/O with dense compute; they could not. This is the one place where 4 GB VRAM + 12 GB RAM + NVMe is architecturally better than a 48 GB M3 Max, and the design should exploit it aggressively — it is what makes the §08 prefetch pipeline worth building.

Live risks

  • Cache capacity may be too small. 11.7% of experts at 4-bit. If oracle hit rate at that size is low, 4-bit becomes unaffordable and you are pushed to 2-bit — into the quality regime Flash-MoE flagged. E3 resolves this and it is the single highest-information experiment available.
  • Windows I/O. Every cited result is Linux or macOS. io_uring has no Windows equivalent; you need overlapped I/O with FILE_FLAG_NO_BUFFERING, and Windows' page cache behaves differently under memory pressure. Budget real time, or develop under WSL2 and accept its filesystem penalty.
  • 4 GB VRAM is genuinely tight. The 2.13 GiB dense core assumes 3-bit GDN. If GDN needs 4-bit for quality, that becomes 2.4 GiB and the VRAM hot-expert tier nearly disappears. Not fatal — CPU execution is the default path anyway — but it removes an optimization.
  • Speculative prefetch may not pay. Cross-layer routing correlation in a 512-expert top-10 router is unmeasured. If accuracy is near chance, prefetch causes evictions and net harm. E6 has an explicit kill condition; honor it.

Research gaps nobody appears to have filled

  • No published expert-locality characterization for a 512-expert top-10 router at <15% cache capacity. The 56–64% figures come from a different model at a far more generous ratio. This is a clean, publishable measurement and you are well positioned to make it.
  • No measurement of cross-layer routing predictability in hybrid GDN/attention MoE stacks — i.e. does layer ℓ's hidden state predict layer ℓ+1's top-k above chance? This determines whether prefetch is viable for a whole class of systems.
  • No quality-vs-bitwidth study that leads with structured output. Flash-MoE observed the JSON failure anecdotally; nobody has curve-fitted tool-call conformance against expert bit-width. Given how much agentic use depends on it, this is the most practically valuable result on this list.
  • No comparison of OS page cache against custom expert caches under 12 GB pressure. Flash-MoE's "trust the OS" finding was on a 48 GB machine with ~35 GB of page cache. Whether it survives at 7 GiB is genuinely open and cheap to test.

Getting the trace is the only hard part, and it is tractable. Routing is a property of the model and the prompt, not of how fast you ran it. A weekend of slow generation produces a trace that answers a question worth a quarter of engineering time.

On the hypothesis itself

You asked me to challenge it. I tried to, and it held: the working set really is decoupled from the model size here, and the numbers say so with more margin than the framing assumed. But the framing has one flaw worth naming. "Treat weights as a virtual address space" implies the runtime's job is paging — reacting to misses. The measurements suggest the opposite emphasis. Flash-MoE's best result came from removing custom paging and letting the OS handle it; #27864's PLE result works because the addresses are knowable in advance; #27712's gain came from bounding residency, not from smarter replacement. The reframing I would offer:

A sparse model is not a virtual address space to be paged. It is a schedule of reads that is largely knowable before it is needed — exactly knowable for PLE, statistically knowable for experts. The engineering problem is issuing those reads early enough, in large enough units, in the right order. Paging is what you do when you failed to schedule.

That reframing is what makes §09 exact rather than speculative, what makes contiguous repacking matter more than clever eviction, and what turns the roadmap from "build a cache" into "build a profiler, then decide." It is also, I think, where the defensible novelty is.
